\section{The problem}

I will point a counterexample against Kakeya's conjecture for~$n=2$.

\textbf{Kakeya's conjecture for $n=2$.} A union of unit-length intervals on a plane has Hausdorff dimension~$2$, if there are intervals of all possible directions among them.

The counterexample:

Let $x\mapsto u(x)=(x_0,x_1,\dots,)$ be any of (many) mappings from $x\in[0;1]$ to its binary represantation, i.e. $x_i\in\{0,1\}$, $u(x)=\sum_{i=1}^\infty \frac{x_i}{2^i}$.

Then define $c(x)=\sum_{i=1}^\infty \frac{2x_i}{3^i}$. It is well known that $c(x)\in C$ where $C$ is the Cantor set.

The counterexample is the union
\begin{equation}\label{maindef}
U=\bigcup_{x\in[0;1[}S_x
\end{equation}
of intervals \[ S_x = \setcond{(c(x)-t\cos(\pi x), t\sin(\pi x))}{t\in\left]0;1\right[}. \]

\section{The proof}

Obviously, each interval~$S_x$ is of length~$1$.

Obviously, $U$~contains intervals of all directions.

% First, it is easy to see that segments~$S_x$ don't intersect each other.

\begin{rem}
The further proof is based mainly on Grok 3 AI output (checked by me), that saved me time instead of writing the proof by myself.
\end{rem}

Partition $\left[0;1\right[$ into~$2^m$ intervals $I_s=\left[\frac{k}{2^m};\frac{k+1}{2^m}\right[$, $k=0,\ldots,2^m-1$, each of length $1/2^m$, binary digits $s=(s_1,\ldots,s_m)$ (the first~$m$ binary digits of a number). Define $U_s=\bigcup_{x\in I_s} S_x$, so by~\eqref{maindef} we have $U=\bigcup_s U_s$.

For $x\in I_x$, express $c(x)=a_s+r$, where $a_s=\sum_{i=1}^m \frac{2s_i}{3^i}$ and \[r=\sum_{i=m+1}^\infty \frac{2x_i}{3^i}.\] We have $r\leq 1/3^m$ by properties of ternary numbers.
 so $c(x)\in[a_s;a_s+1/3^m]$. Approximate~$U_s$ with a paralellogram~$T_s$:
\[
T_s = \bigcup_{c\in[a_s;a_s+1/3^m]} \setcond{(c(x_s)-t\cos(\pi x_s), c(x_s)+t\sin(\pi x_s)}{t\in[0;1]},
\]
where $x_s=k/2^m$. This paralellogram has base $1/3^m$ and height $\lvert\sin(\pi x_s)\rvert$ with area $O(1/3^m)$.

For $x\in I_s$, $\lvert\cos(\pi x)-\cos(\pi x_s)\rvert$ and $\lvert\sin(\pi x)-\sin(\pi x_s)\rvert$ are (mean value theorem) $O(1/3^m)$, so $U_s$ lies within a $\delta$-neigh\-bor\-hood of~$T_s$, with $\delta=O(1/2^m)$, because:
\begin{multline*}
\lvert(c(x_s)-t\cos(\pi x_s), c+t\sin(\pi x_s) - ((c(x)-t\cos(\pi x), t\sin(\pi x)))\rvert=\\
\lvert((c(x_s)-c(x))-t(\cos(\pi x)-\cos(\pi x_s), t(\sin(\pi x_s)-\sin(\pi x)))\rvert=O(1/3^m),
\end{multline*}
because $c(x_s)-c(x)=O(1/3^m)$ and $\lvert\cos(\pi x)-\cos(\pi x_s)\rvert$ and $\lvert\sin(\pi x)-\sin(\pi x_s)\rvert$ are $O(1/2^m)$,
$\lvert t\rvert\leq 1$.

Further, split $T_s$ into smaller paralellograms:
\[
T_{sl} = \bigcup_{c\in[a_s;a_s+1/3^m]} \setcond{(c(x_s)-t\cos(\pi x_s), c(x_s)+t\sin(\pi x_s))}{t\in\left[\frac{l}{2^m};\frac{l+1}{2^m}\right[},
\]
where $l=0,\ldots,3^m-1$.
Each such a paralellogram~$T_{sl}$ can be covered by $O(1)$ balls of radius~$1/(2^m3^m)$ (because it fits inside a rectangle of height~$2/3^m$ and width~$2/2^m$).

[[[

Consequently, $T_s$ can be covered by $O(2^m)$ balls of radius~$1/(2^m3^m)$, or what is the same by $O(2^{2m})$ balls of radius~$1/3^m$.

Cover each~$T_s$ with balls of diameter $\epsilon=1/3^m$. The number of balls is $O(\mathrm{area}/\epsilon^2)$. Including the neighborhood, adjust the cover for~$U_s$, but the key is the asymptotic cost:

\begin{itemize}
\item Number of~$U_s$: $2^m$,
\item Balls per~$U_s$: $O(3^m)$,
\item Sum of~$(\diam)^2$: $2^m\cdot O(3^m)\cdot(1/3^m)^2=O((2/3)^m)$.
\end{itemize}

]]]

As $m\to\infty$, $(2/3)^m\to 0$ and $\epsilon\to 0$, so the Hausdorff measure $H^2(U)=0$.

So $\dim U<2$.
